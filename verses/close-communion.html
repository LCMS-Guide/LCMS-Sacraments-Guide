Close Communion: The Verses

The Bible passages behind close communion, explained in the LCMS’s own words.

How to use this page

The case rests on the Bible passage at the top of each card. Under it are the LCMS’s own words, quoted exactly, with the source under each quote.

Someone raised an objection? Tap it in the list below to open the LCMS’s answer.

Lines marked Note are not quotations. They explain a word or link two quotes.

Most quotes are from reports of the LCMS’s theology commission, the CTCR. The LCMS’s standard is Scripture. It holds that the Lutheran Confessions explain Scripture correctly.

Words used on this page

Confession: what a person or church publicly says it believes. It does not mean confessing sins.

Confessor: a person seen as holding the teaching of the church he or she belongs to.

The Lutheran Confessions: the Lutheran statements of faith from the 1500s, such as the Small Catechism, the Augsburg Confession, and the Formula of Concord. The LCMS holds that they explain Scripture correctly.

The Synod: the LCMS. The CTCR is its Commission on Theology and Church Relations.

Eucharist, Sacrament of the Altar, Holy Communion: other names for the Lord’s Supper.

Real Presence: Christ’s true body and blood are present in the Supper and given with the bread and wine. Other Christians use the phrase with other meanings.

Words of institution: Jesus’ words at the Last Supper: “This is my body… This is my blood.”

Corporate: of the whole congregation together.

Altar fellowship, church fellowship: churches sharing Communion and preaching because they agree in what they teach.

Heterodox: the LCMS’s word for a church whose public teaching differs in important ways from what Scripture teaches. It does not mean its members are not Christians. With a small o, “orthodox” means teaching rightly.

Koinonia: the Greek word translated “participation,” “communion,” or “fellowship.”

What close communion is

“Members in good standing of the LCMS and her sister church bodies may commune at LCMS congregations. This practice, known variably as close, closed or close(d) Communion, stands in contrast to open Communion, whereby anyone who desires may commune regardless of profession of faith or member status. By rejecting the practice of open Communion, pastors and communing laity alike preserve a unity of confession regarding the Sacrament, namely, that in, with and under the elements Christ is present as He promised for the forgiveness of sins. Pastors may, nevertheless, exercise discretion in special cases or emergencies.”

lcms.org, “Who can commune at a Lutheran Church—Missouri Synod congregation?”

Note “Confession” on this page means what a person or church publicly says it believes, not confessing sins. “Sister church bodies” are churches that have formally agreed with the LCMS in doctrine. Many churches that welcome all believers also ask for faith and self-examination. The LCMS still differs with them, over what the Supper is and over unity of confession.

“We do this not with any thought of superiority or with any gladness, but in deep sorrow and prayer to the Lord of the Church that he would remove the divisions that separate His children from one another in their visible expressions of fellowship.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Concluding Summary, on asking Christians of another confession not to commune at LCMS altars

Note The practice goes both ways. See “Can a Lutheran take Communion at another church?”

How the LCMS makes its case

Note The 1999 report says plainly what 1 Corinthians 10–11 does and does not address, and then gives its conclusion.

“In 1 Corinthians 10 and 11 Paul does not speak to the same denominational situations as exist today because he is addressing an intra-congregational situation. As indicated above, however, the Scriptures do address doctrinal divisions very much like those that mark the contemporary scene. While ‘denominational membership’ cannot become an automatic litmus test for admission to the Sacrament, nevertheless the Lord’s Supper presumes and requires a unity (Acts 2:42) that is contradicted when, under ordinary circumstances, opposing confessions are represented among those who commune together.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part III

“There are two reasons why people can be refused admission to the Lord’s Supper. The first has to do with faith and discerning the body. Those who do not have such faith and discernment would commune in an unworthy manner and thereby receive God’s judgment. But the second reason has to do with the need for a fitting confessional unity among those who commune together.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Appendix, Question 1

Note “Intra-congregational” means within one congregation. Parts 1 and 4 below are about the first reason. Parts 2 and 3 are about the second.

1. What the Supper is

“This is my body… this is my blood” Matthew 26:26–28

26Now as they were eating, Jesus took bread, and after blessing it broke it and gave it to the disciples, and said, “Take, eat; this is my body.” 27And he took a cup, and when he had given thanks he gave it to them, saying, “Drink of it, all of you, 28for this is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins.ESV

See it in context

What the LCMS says

“The clear claim of Christ in Holy Scripture is that His true body and blood are truly present and distributed to those who eat. All four Scriptural accounts (Matt. 26:26-29; Mark 14:22-25; Luke 22:15-20; 1 Cor. 11:23-26) assert: ‘This is my body.’”

LCMS CTCR, Theology and Practice of the Lord’s Supper (1983), Part I, C.1

“It is the true body and blood of our Lord Jesus Christ, under the bread and wine, for us Christians to eat and to drink, instituted by Christ Himself.”

Lutheran Confessions: Small Catechism, The Sacrament of the Altar

Objection“Jesus also said ‘I am the door’ and ‘I am the vine.’ Isn’t ‘This is my body’ a figure of speech too?”
The LCMS answers

“here, as in the making of His last will and testament and of His everabiding covenant and union … He uses not allegorical, but entirely proper, simple, indubitable, and clear words; and in order that no misunderstanding can occur, He explains them more clearly with the words: Given for you, shed for you.”

Lutheran Confessions: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 50

“all three evangelists, Matt. 26:26; Mark 14:22; Luke 22:19, and St. Paul … unanimously and with the same words and syllables repeat concerning the consecrated and distributed bread these distinct, clear, firm, and true words of Christ: This is My body, altogether in one way, without any interpretation [trope, figure] and change.”

Lutheran Confessions: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 52

“Lutheran Christians firmly confess that the ‘true’ body and blood—that is, the real, actual body and blood of our Lord Jesus Christ—are what Christ gives Christians to eat and to drink. Any talk of Jesus’ words as symbolic or mere figures of speech is ruled out.”

LCMS CTCR, Proper Administration of the Lord’s Supper (2025), Theological Considerations

Note None of these documents takes up “I am the door” or “I am the vine” as an objection. A “trope” is a figure of speech.

Objection“So Lutherans believe the bread turns into Jesus’ body?”
The LCMS answers

“it is also fruitless to engage in theories about how the body and blood are present in, with, and under the bread and wine. A dogma such as transubstantiation, as generally taught by Roman Catholicism, is not set forth by Scripture.”

LCMS CTCR, Theology and Practice of the Lord’s Supper (1983), Part I, C.1

Note Transubstantiation is the teaching that the bread and wine change into Christ’s body and blood and stop being bread and wine. Lutherans say “in, with, and under”: the bread is still bread, and Christ’s body is truly given with it.

↑ Back to the list

Objection“Jesus said, ‘Do this in remembrance of me.’ Doesn’t that make it a memorial?”
The LCMS answers

“this most venerable Sacrament, which was to be used until the end of the world with great reverence and obedience [and humility], and was to be an abiding memorial of His bitter suffering and death and all His benefits, a sealing [and confirmation] of the New Testament, a consolation of all distressed hearts, and a firm bond of union of Christians with Christ, their Head, and with one another”

Lutheran Confessions: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 44

“to remember Christ is not the idle celebration of a show … but it is to remember the benefits of Christ and receive them by faith, so as to be quickened by them.”

Lutheran Confessions: Apology of the Augsburg Confession, Article XXIV, paragraph 72

Note So the LCMS does not deny that the Supper is a remembrance. It says the Supper is also what Christ’s words call it: His body and blood. “Quickened” means made alive. The words “in remembrance of me” are in Luke 22:19 and 1 Corinthians 11:24–25.

“A participation in the body of Christ” 1 Corinthians 10:16

16The cup of blessing that we bless, is it not a participation in the blood of Christ? The bread that we break, is it not a participation in the body of Christ?ESV

See it in context

What the LCMS says

“Paul explains why the Corinthian Christians cannot partake of the sacrifices at idol shrines while also eating the Lord’s Supper. After establishing that their eating and drinking is, in fact, participation in the body and the blood of the Lord (10:16), Paul continues: ‘Because there is one loaf, we many are one body, for we all partake of the one loaf’ (10:17).”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part I, A

“From this we clearly learn that not only the cup which Christ blessed at the first Supper, and not only the bread which Christ broke and distributed, but also that which we break and bless, is the communion of the body and blood of Christ, so that all who eat this bread and drink of this cup truly receive, and are partakers of, the true body and blood of Christ.”

Lutheran Confessions: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 54, on 1 Corinthians 10:16

Note “Participation” translates the Greek word koinonia, also translated “communion” or “fellowship.” The LCMS’s 1981 report on fellowship says its root meaning is “having part in a common thing” (Part I, A.1).

Objection“Doesn’t ‘participation’ just mean spiritual fellowship with Christ by faith?”
The LCMS answers

“For since Paul says, 1 Cor. 10:16, that the bread is the communion of the Lord’s body, etc., it would follow, if the Lord’s body were not truly present, that the bread is not a communion of the body, but only of the spirit of Christ.”

Lutheran Confessions: Apology of the Augsburg Confession, Article X, paragraph 54

“For since all those who become partakers of the consecrated and broken bread in the Supper have communion also with the body of Christ, St. Paul indeed cannot be speaking of spiritual communion with Christ, which no man can abuse, and against which also no one is to be warned.”

Lutheran Confessions: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 57

↑ Back to the list

Objection“Isn’t 1 Corinthians 10 about idols and unbelievers, not about Communion between Christians?”
The LCMS answers

“This is shown also by the causes and circumstances of this entire exposition of St. Paul, in which he deters and warns those who ate of offerings to idols and had fellowship with heathen devil-worship, and nevertheless went also to the table of the Lord and became partakers of the body and blood of Christ, lest they receive the body and blood of Christ for judgment and condemnation to themselves.”

Lutheran Confessions: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 57

Note So the LCMS agrees that the setting is idol worship. Its point is what Paul says about the Lord’s Supper along the way. The first quote on this card says the same. The 2014 guidelines apply the warning in 1 Corinthians 10:14–22 to people who are not baptized, believing Christians (endnote 9). None of the documents quoted on this page calls another Christian church’s Communion “the table of demons” (verse 21). Walther does list that verse, in passing, when he calls the Supper one of the “seals of the church’s doctrine and faith” (in the pamphlet quoted on the next card).

2. Receiving it together is a confession of one faith

“One bread… one body” 1 Corinthians 10:17

17Because there is one bread, we who are many are one body, for we all partake of the one bread.ESV

See it in context

What the LCMS says

“As there is but one bread, one loaf, from which we eat, so we who are eating of this loaf are one body. The eating of one and the same loaf of bread unifies us to one body. Our participation in the Lord’s Supper is a public profession on our part that we are not only in fellowship with Christ, but that we also are in fellowship with those with whom we commune at the Lord’s Table. … Upon this Bible passage do we base the saying, ‘Altar Fellowship is Church Fellowship.’”

LCMS President A. L. Barry, What About… Fellowship in the Lord’s Supper, quoting Stoeckhardt, 1 Corinthians

“The Lord’s body in the Supper actually effects—that is, maintains—the oneness of the body of believers, the Christians who eat the bread.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part I, A

“In whichever church one receives the Holy Supper, one is confessing that church and its doctrine.”

LCMS President A. L. Barry, What About… Fellowship in the Lord’s Supper, quoting C. F. W. Walther, Pastoral Theology

Objection“Aren’t we one body when we take it in remembrance of Jesus Christ?”
The LCMS answers

“As members of the one holy Christian church they are also one with every other Christian who lives or who has ever lived on the face of the earth.”

LCMS CTCR, Concept of Fellowship report (1981), Part I, B, Principle 1, speaking of all Christians

“According to the Scriptures, external unity in the church is a matter of the right confession of the prophetic and apostolic faith.”

LCMS CTCR, Concept of Fellowship report (1981), Part I, B, Principle 7

“Since fellowship at the Lord’s Table is also confession of a common faith, it would not be truthful for those who affirm the Real Presence and those who deny it to join one another.”

LCMS CTCR, Theology and Practice of the Lord’s Supper (1983), Part II, D.2

Note So the answer has two halves. Yes, all believers are one in Christ. And sharing the altar says something more: that we confess the same faith.

“You proclaim the Lord’s death” 1 Corinthians 11:26

26For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes.ESV

See it in context

What the LCMS says

“The Lord’s Supper, Paul declares, is a joint proclamation of the Lord’s death, that is, of the nature and benefits of the Lord’s death.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part I, A

“The Lord’s Supper was the communicants’ remembering of Jesus’ work on their behalf. That is to say, they were proclaiming to one another its meaning and its benefits.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part I, A

“The church confesses its faith in Christ and the Gospel as it worships and as it communes.”

LCMS CTCR, Guidelines for Communion Statements (2014), Theological Components, 3.a, citing 1 Corinthians 11:26

“The person whose confession of faith (his or her personal beliefs and/or the public confession made through his or her affiliation with a particular church body) is contrary to the communing assembly (‘to the faith which is confessed at this altar’) introduces the possibility of doubt, confusion, and potentially harmful division if he or she communes.”

LCMS CTCR, Guidelines for Communion Statements (2014), Theological Components, 3.c, citing 1 Corinthians 11:26; 1:10; Ephesians 4:1–3; Philippians 1:27

Objection“Communion is personal, between you and God. So you should be able to take it at another church.”
The LCMS answers

“While the Lord’s Supper is always a personal matter, it is never a private matter. That is an important truth that is often overlooked. Those who commune at the same altar are thereby declaring publically that they are united in the doctrine of the Apostles (Acts 2:42). Therefore, fellowship in the Supper is church fellowship.”

LCMS President A. L. Barry, What About… Fellowship in the Lord’s Supper

“When individual communicants approach the Lord’s Supper, it is not only a matter between themselves and their Lord. Rather, the relationship of love and unity with the fellow communicant is equally in view and must also be preserved with equal dedication.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Appendix, Question 8, on the passing of the peace before Communion

“The apostles’ teaching and the fellowship… the breaking of bread” Acts 2:42

42And they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers.ESV

See it in context

What the LCMS says

“Should Christians who have differing confessions commune at the same table? While the New Testament documents do not specifically discuss this issue, we do have the strong positive statement regarding the early church in Acts 2:42 … The reference to a common life devoted to a common teaching is explicit, and it was in such a context that the celebration of the Lord’s Supper took place.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part I, C

Objection“‘Breaking of bread’ could just be an ordinary meal.”
The LCMS answers

“That the phrase ‘the breaking of the bread’ here refers to the Lord’s Supper is noted even by scholars with no particularly high sacramental theology of their own. … Alford himself nuances the view by understanding ‘the breaking of the bread’ as a reference to the Christians’ community meals, ‘accompanied as they were at this time by the celebration of the Lord’s Supper’”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), note 40, which cites F. F. Bruce and Henry Alford

“the Scriptures place ‘the breaking of bread’ at the center of worship (Acts 2:42; 20:7; cf. 1 Cor. 11:20, 33).”

LCMS CTCR, Theology and Practice of the Lord’s Supper (1983), Part III, Question 11, on how often to offer the Supper

3. The unity Scripture calls for

“Baptized into one body” 1 Corinthians 12:13

13For in one Spirit we were all baptized into one body—Jews or Greeks, slaves or free—and all were made to drink of one Spirit.ESV

See it in context

What the LCMS says

“In Baptism, we are united with Christ and thereby also united with every other baptized Christian. We share the same promises and the same hope as part of the same Body of Christ. Herein lies our indissoluble unity as Christians.”

LCMS CTCR, An Inexpressible Treasure (2018), section “Baptism Makes Us Members of a New Community,” on 1 Corinthians 12:12–13

“Since the one holy Christian church (una sancta ecclesia) includes believers in Christ, its members are to be found throughout the world in Christian denominations where the Gospel is taught and the sacraments are administered.”

LCMS CTCR, Concept of Fellowship report (1981), Part I, A.2

Objection“If we are one body, then refusing to commune together divides the body.”
The LCMS answers

“While it is a possibility that an individual Christian may be personally prepared to receive the Lord’s Supper worthily and to his or her own personal blessing, we may not classify Christians only as individuals. Rather, all Christians adhere to a confession (or choose to reject all formal confessions), and the terrible doctrinal divisions in the visible church must, tragically, be reflected in the teaching concerning admission to the Lord’s Supper.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part II, A

“Close Communion seeks to prevent a profession of confessional unity in faith where there is, in fact, disunity and disagreement.”

LCMS CTCR, Theology and Practice of the Lord’s Supper (1983), Part II, D.2

Note So the LCMS’s answer is that the division is already there, in what the churches teach. Its practice at the altar reflects that division. It does not create it.

“One Lord, one faith, one baptism” Ephesians 4:1–6

1I therefore, a prisoner for the Lord, urge you to walk in a manner worthy of the calling to which you have been called, 2with all humility and gentleness, with patience, bearing with one another in love, 3eager to maintain the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace. 4There is one body and one Spirit—just as you were called to the one hope that belongs to your call— 5one Lord, one faith, one baptism, 6one God and Father of all, who is over all and through all and in all.ESV

See it in context

What the LCMS says

“The Scriptures, therefore, exhort Christians to manifest the unity which has already been given them by virtue of their incorporation into the body of Christ.”

LCMS CTCR, Concept of Fellowship report (1981), Part I, A.3, which then quotes Ephesians 4:1–3

“to the true unity of the Church it is enough to agree concerning the doctrine of the Gospel and the administration of the Sacraments.”

Lutheran Confessions: Augsburg Confession, Article VII, which then quotes Ephesians 4:5–6

Note The LCMS reads “the doctrine of the Gospel” here as the whole of Christian teaching, not a short summary. The first objection below gives its reason. The same 1981 footnote adds that this article “is not in the first instance a programmatic statement for the establishment of denominational fellowship” (note 3). That is, it was not written as a rule for which churches may share Communion.

Objection“We agree on the gospel. Isn’t that enough?”
The LCMS answers

“All articles of faith are integrally related to the Gospel and articulate the Gospel from different perspectives. Consequently the preaching of the Gospel according to a pure understanding of it is not possible when any article of faith is either falsified or denied.”

LCMS CTCR, Concept of Fellowship report (1981), note 3, quoting the CTCR’s 1967 report on what a doctrine is

“the churches will not condemn one another because of dissimilarity of ceremonies when, in Christian liberty, one has less or more of them, provided they are otherwise agreed with one another in the doctrine and all its articles, also in the right use of the holy Sacraments”

Lutheran Confessions: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration X, paragraph 31

Note “Articles” are the teachings of the faith, so “all its articles” means all of them. The 2014 guidelines give Baptism as an example of a difference that matters at the altar (comments on Sample Statement 3).

↑ Back to the list

Objection“Sharing Communion would help bring us together, even while we disagree.”
The LCMS answers

“No. The Confessions rightly teach that Eucharistic fellowship is a thankful celebration of that unity which God has bestowed in the Gospel rather than a device to advance Christian fraternity”

LCMS CTCR, Theology and Practice of the Lord’s Supper (1983), Part III, Question 2: “Is it proper to celebrate Communion as a device for furthering or attaining pulpit or altar fellowship?”

“The refusal of church fellowship for any reason other than disagreement in the confession of the faith … violates the law of Christian love, even as does all loveless and misguided ‘concern’ for the truth. Separatism (sectarianism) is condemned by God’s Word.”

LCMS CTCR, Concept of Fellowship report (1981), Part I, B, Principle 9

“we are not permitted by Christ or His Gospel to hold back and wait for those whom we believe to be in error to come to us. We must go to them, committed to genuine, respectful participation in substantial theological dialogue”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Concluding Summary

“United in the same mind and the same judgment” 1 Corinthians 1:10

10I appeal to you, brothers, by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that all of you agree, and that there be no divisions among you, but that you be united in the same mind and the same judgment.ESV

See it in context

What the LCMS says

“The entirety of Paul’s first Corinthian letter reveals that the Corinthian Christians were a divided, fractured group.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part I, A

“Since it is faith in the heart which binds believers together with Christ and with one another, no human eye can see this spiritual unity. But there is an inner dynamic to faith in Jesus Christ which works toward an external unity embracing all those who confess faith in Jesus Christ (1 Cor. 1:10). What the church is by God’s design is what He wants the church to show itself to be—one—so that ‘the world may believe’ (John 17:21).”

LCMS CTCR, Concept of Fellowship report (1981), Part I, A.3

“It is not the Lord’s supper that you eat” 1 Corinthians 11:18–20

18For, in the first place, when you come together as a church, I hear that there are divisions among you. And I believe it in part, 19for there must be factions among you in order that those who are genuine among you may be recognized. 20When you come together, it is not the Lord's supper that you eat.ESV

See it in context

What the LCMS says

“The closest modern analogy to Paul’s tactics and teaching in 1 Corinthians 11 would be a situation of pastoral care within a particular Christian congregation.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part I, A

“the divisiveness of the Corinthian Christians in their eucharistic assemblies was such a heinous affront precisely because the Lord’s Supper is divinely given to maintain and preserve spiritual unity among those who gather. To gather in disunity, then, is to contradict directly the very nature of the Sacrament and the purpose for which it exists.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part I, A

“Remarkably, yet fittingly, Paul responded with the doctrine of the Sacrament of the Altar, with the declaration of what the Eucharist actually is. For Paul the primary sin, the fundamental issue, was not their abuse of one another, but their abuse of the Lord’s Supper.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part I, A

Objection“Do you think all of them were united? They were divided, and they still took Communion.”
The LCMS answers

“the schisms and factions present at the celebration of the Eucharist (1 Corinthians 11) did not nullify or invalidate the Supper. Such divisions, however, contradicted the Gospel and turned the Lord’s Supper from Christ’s gracious gift into Christ’s presence of judgment.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part I, A

“The most drastic abuses of the Lord’s Supper have resulted in divine judgment and those abuses must stop at once. But as long as the lines of communication are open, as long as the Corinthians acknowledge that Paul is indeed ‘called, an apostle of Christ Jesus through the will of God’ (1 Cor. 1:1), Paul regards them as those who confess his doctrine and he deals with them primarily as individuals.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part I, C

“But 1 Corinthians 11 does not directly speak to the issue of ‘outsiders’ or visitors, of Christians who have stated their allegiance to a different and erring confession of the Gospel and its truth.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part I, B

Note So the LCMS agrees they were divided and still communed. Paul told them it was bringing judgment and had to stop, and he treated them as one church under his own teaching. What this means for Christians of another confession is the LCMS’s conclusion, not a statement in this passage.

Objection“The divisions in Corinth were about rich and poor, not doctrine.”
The LCMS answers

“Paul’s instructions and warnings about ‘divisions’ at the Lord’s Supper (1 Cor. 11:17–34) probably do not have in mind divisions of a doctrinal nature.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part I, C

“The Corinthian Christians clearly did have divisions of a doctrinal character that included following after different teachings (1 Cor. 1:10), rejecting apostolic teaching regarding morality (1 Corinthians 5), abuses in the context of worship (1 Corinthians 12–14), different views on the resurrection (1 Corinthians 15), etc.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), note 35

Note So the report separates the two. The divisions at the Supper in chapter 11 were probably social. Elsewhere in the letter Paul also corrects divisions over doctrine.

“Differences of confession cannot be a matter of indifference when seeking the unity presupposed by the Lord’s Supper, the very unity that the Supper is given to maintain and preserve.”

Question“Does the Bible directly command close communion?”
The LCMS answers

“Fifth and finally, the crucial passage, 1 Cor. 11:17–34, places only a minor emphasis (at least explicitly) on the need for doctrinal unity among those who commune together. But we must remember that Paul is dealing with an intra-congregational situation and that elsewhere in the letter he corrects the Corinthians’ errors in matters of both doctrine and personal morality.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part II, C

“How would Paul think about communion fellowship with ‘outsiders,’ that is, with Christians who specifically rejected his doctrine of Baptism or Scripture or the Lord’s Supper itself? Again, we must speak carefully since no Pauline text brings these two issues together in explicit terms. But this much is clear. Paul did not tolerate doctrinal diversity. His teaching was that of one sent by Christ Himself and his hearers were to accept it as such. Moreover, the Eucharist was the congregation’s sacrament of unity, and divisions were to be dealt with before mutual communing took place. The biblical evidence leads to this conclusion: unity in apostolic doctrine is appropriate and necessary among those who commune together at the Lord’s Supper.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part I, C

“The refusal to affirm church fellowship (in the sense of external unity in the church) with those who do not confess the faith … as it is taught in the Scriptures is not an optional matter but a Scriptural mandate”

LCMS CTCR, Concept of Fellowship report (1981), Part I, B, Principle 8, citing Romans 16:17–20 and other passages

Note So the 1999 report grants that no passage discusses, in so many words, Christians of differing confessions at one table. It still concludes that unity in doctrine is “appropriate and necessary” there. The 1981 report states the wider principle more strongly, as a mandate of Scripture. “Intra-congregational” means within one congregation.

Question, in the LCMS’s own wording“How can we possibly say that all those Christians from other church bodies are unworthy to receive the Lord’s Supper? Isn’t that what we are saying?”

Not answered in these documents

None of the documents quoted on this page takes up these two:

  • Romans 15:7, “welcome one another as Christ has welcomed you.”
  • How much the disciples themselves understood at the Last Supper.

They are good questions to take to a pastor.
