This Is My Body

Why Lutherans do not read the Lord’s Supper as only symbolic: the Bible passages, explained in the words of The Lutheran Church—Missouri Synod (LCMS).

How to use this page

This page does not ask anyone to agree. It shows why Lutherans read these passages the way they do, so that the reasoning can be seen, and it says where the LCMS documents give no answer.

Each card has a Bible passage, then the LCMS’s own words, quoted exactly, with the source under each quote. Have a question or an objection? Find it in the list below and tap it. The objections were worded for this page, as fairly as it could be done.

Three kinds of lines are not quotations. Note explains a word or links two quotes. Added fact gives a fact from outside the LCMS documents, such as a Greek word or a date. Not addressed marks a point on which these documents are silent.

The LCMS’s standard is Scripture. The Lutheran Confessions, written in the 1500s, are quoted to show what Lutherans confess, not as proof. Some of their sentences use sharp words about other churches; this page leaves out the sharpest of them.

Jump to an objection or question (17)

1. Jesus’ words: “This is my body”

  • ‘This is my body’ is a figure of speech, like ‘I am the door.’
  • In the Bible ‘is’ often means ‘represents.’
  • Jesus was right there at the table.
  • Jesus still called it ‘fruit of the vine.’
  • ‘Do this in remembrance of me’ makes it a memorial.
  • We only remember someone who is absent.
  • ‘This cup is the new covenant’ can’t be literal.

2. Paul’s explanation

  • ‘Participation’ means fellowship by faith, or with the church.
  • Dishonoring a symbol can still be a serious sin.
  • ‘Discerning the body’ means recognizing the church.

3. What the Supper gives

  • Isn’t that sacrificing Christ again?
  • ‘For the forgiveness of sins’ describes the cross, not the Supper.
  • If faith in the words is enough, what does the body add?

4. John 6 and Christ in heaven

  • ‘The flesh is no help at all’ (John 6:63).
  • This sounds like cannibalism.
  • Christ’s body is in heaven, so it can’t be in the bread.
  • Believers feed on Christ by the Spirit; unbelievers get only bread.
Words used on this page

LCMS: The Lutheran Church—Missouri Synod. The CTCR is its Commission on Theology and Church Relations.

Lord’s Supper, Holy Communion, Eucharist, Sacrament of the Altar: names for the same meal. “Eucharist” comes from the Greek for “He gave thanks.”

Words of institution: Jesus’ words when He gave the Supper: “This is my body… This is my blood.”

Real presence: the teaching that Christ’s true body and blood are present in the Supper. Lutherans, Roman Catholics, and the Orthodox all hold it, and explain it differently. Reformed Christians speak of a real spiritual presence.

Sacramental union: the Lutheran term for how bread and body, wine and blood, are joined in the Supper. The bread stays bread.

Memorial view: the teaching that the bread and wine are signs that help believers remember Christ’s death.

Spiritual presence: the teaching, from Calvin, that believers are truly fed with Christ’s body and blood by the Holy Spirit, while His body remains in heaven.

Transubstantiation: the Roman Catholic teaching that the bread and wine change into Christ’s body and blood and stop being bread and wine. Lutherans reject it.

Consubstantiation: a label often given to the Lutheran view. The LCMS rejects the label.

Reformed: churches in the line of Zwingli and Calvin, such as Presbyterians. The Cyclopedia shortens it to “Ref.”

Sacrament: a sacred act Christ commanded, in which His promise is joined to something physical.

Means of grace: the ways God gives forgiveness and faith: His Word, Baptism, and the Lord’s Supper.

The Lutheran Confessions: the Lutheran statements of faith from the 1500s, such as the Small and Large Catechisms, the Apology of the Augsburg Confession, and the Formula of Concord. The LCMS holds that they explain Scripture correctly.

Ap, FC, SD, Ep: short forms for the Apology of the Augsburg Confession and the Formula of Concord, with its two parts, the Solid Declaration and the Epitome.

LSB: in the translation menu, the Legacy Standard Bible.

Where we agree

Note Christians who hold the memorial view and Lutherans agree on a great deal. Jesus commanded the Supper. It is a remembrance of His death (Luke 22:19) and proclaims it until He comes (1 Corinthians 11:26). His death was the one sacrifice for sin, once for all (Hebrews 10:10–14). Those who come should examine themselves (1 Corinthians 11:28). And Christ is received by faith.

Note The LCMS Cyclopedia puts the difference this way. “Ref.” means Reformed Christians, who hold a spiritual presence, and “Luths.” means Lutherans. Those who hold the memorial view may not accept all of what it lists.

“The question is not whether Christ is present acc. to His divine nature in the Sacrament, or whether the soul by faith is united with Christ (spiritual eating and drinking), or whether the believing communicant receives the merits of Christ’s shed blood by faith (all of which is acknowledged as true by both Luths. and Ref.).”

LCMS, Christian Cyclopedia (Concordia Publishing House), “Grace, Means of,” IV, “Lord’s Supper as a means of grace,” section 3 (Real Presence)

Why Lutherans don’t read it as only symbolic

Note The reasons the LCMS documents give, summed up in the page’s words with short official phrases. After each is the reading of those who hold the memorial view, and a link to the card where both are shown in full.

  1. Jesus said “is,” and did not explain it as a picture. All four accounts say “This is my body,” and none adds “this represents.” Lutherans hold that His words are “not to be understood otherwise than as they read” (Formula of Concord).
    The other reading: “is” can mean “represents,” as in Genesis 41:26. Part 1 ↓
  2. Lutherans point to the setting: a meal and a last will. He handed them bread, said “Take, eat,” and added “given for you.” From that setting the Formula of Concord concludes that “there can be no metaphor” here, and that “He does not speak concerning a sign of His body” (paragraphs 48–49).
    The other reading: the Passover Jesus was keeping was itself a meal of explained symbols (Exodus 12:26–27). Part 1 ↓
  3. Lutherans read remembrance and “This is my body” together. They come one right after the other in Luke 22:19. In the LCMS’s words, “Do this in remembrance of me” does “not mean merely that the communicant is to remember the absent Christ” (Christian Cyclopedia).
    The other reading: remembering is the purpose of the meal. Part 1 ↓
  4. Lutherans note that Paul calls the bread a sharing in Christ’s body (1 Corinthians 10:16), not only a reminder of it. If Christ’s body were not present, the Apology reasons, “the bread is not a communion of the body, but only of the spirit of Christ” (Article X).
    The other reading: “participation” means fellowship with Christ by faith, or with His people (verse 17). Part 2 ↓
  5. Paul says that receiving it wrongly makes a person guilty concerning Christ’s body and blood (1 Corinthians 11:27). The Formula of Concord reads this as a sin “not merely against the signs or symbols and emblems of the body and blood” (paragraph 60).
    The other reading: dishonoring a symbol can dishonor what it stands for. Part 2 ↓
  6. Lutherans reason that a symbol can remind us of a gift, but Christ says this meal gives one: His body “given for you,” His blood “poured out … for the forgiveness of sins” (Luke 22:19; Matthew 26:28). The Small Catechism says that “in the Sacrament forgiveness of sins, life, and salvation are given us through these words” (“Given, and shed for you”).
    The other reading: “for the forgiveness of sins” describes what His blood did on the cross. Part 3 ↓
  7. Many early Christians spoke of it this way. Justin Martyr, about AD 155: “not as common bread and common drink do we receive these” (not an LCMS document; evidence, not proof).
    The other reading: other early writers, such as Tertullian and Augustine, used the language of figure and sign. Part 5 ↓
  8. Lutherans hold that not being able to explain how is not a reason to doubt it. They offer no theory of how Christ is present. Luther in the Large Catechism: “For as the lips of Christ say and speak, so it is, as He can never lie or deceive.”
    The other reading: Christ’s human body, like ours, is in one place, and it is in heaven (Acts 3:21). Part 4 ↓

What Lutherans believe

“It is the true body and blood of our Lord Jesus Christ, under the bread and wine, for us Christians to eat and to drink, instituted by Christ Himself.”

What Lutherans confess: Small Catechism, The Sacrament of the Altar, “What is the Sacrament of the Altar?”

“The Lord’s Supper (also called Communion or the Eucharist) is a Sacrament, a physical means established by Christ to forgive sins, and along with that comes life and salvation, to those who partake of the Supper with faith in Christ and His Word of promise. In the Lord’s Supper, Christ dwells in body and spirit in, with and under the bread and wine, granting forgiveness of sins and life everlasting to those who partake worthily.”

lcms.org, Beliefs, “What is the Lord’s Supper, and what does it do?”

“There is no transubstantiation of the bread and wine into the body and blood of Christ, nor any consubstantiation or impanation.”

LCMS, Christian Cyclopedia (Concordia Publishing House), “Grace, Means of,” IV, “Lord’s Supper as a means of grace,” section 3 (Real Presence)

“View, falsely charged to Lutheranism, that bread and body form 1 substance (a ‘3d substance’) in Communion (similarly wine and blood) or that body and blood are present, like bread and wine, in a natural manner.”

LCMS, Christian Cyclopedia (Concordia Publishing House), “Consubstantiation”

Note So Lutherans do not say the bread changes into Christ’s body, or that bread and body blend into a third thing. They say the bread stays bread and Christ’s true body is truly given with it, because He said so. They do not claim to explain how. “Impanation” is the idea that Christ’s body is enclosed in the bread. “3d” means third.

The four main views

Added fact Memorial view: the bread and wine are signs that help believers remember Christ’s death. Christ’s body is in heaven. This view is often traced to Ulrich Zwingli (1520s).

Added fact Spiritual presence: Christ’s body is in heaven, but believers who eat in faith are truly fed with His body and blood by the Holy Spirit. This is John Calvin’s view and that of many Reformed churches.

Added fact Sacramental union: the Lutheran view described above.

“Term for RC view that in the eucharistic rite the substance or basic reality of bread and wine are changed into the body and blood of Jesus Christ, while the outward appearances of bread and wine are not affected.”

LCMS, Christian Cyclopedia (Concordia Publishing House), “Transubstantiation”

Note That last is transubstantiation, the Roman Catholic (“RC”) view. The Cyclopedia describes where the other views find a figure of speech in Jesus’ words:

“Karlstadt sought the figure in ‘this,’ H. Zwingli in ‘is’ (making ‘is’ mean ‘represents’), J. Calvin and others in ‘body’ (making ‘body’ mean ‘the sign of My body’)”

LCMS, Christian Cyclopedia (Concordia Publishing House), “Grace, Means of,” IV, “Lord’s Supper as a means of grace,” section 3 (Real Presence)

Note Andreas Karlstadt was an early Reformer who broke with Luther. The parts below show the Lutheran reasons in full: Jesus’ own words (Part 1), Paul’s explanation (Part 2), what the Supper gives (Part 3), John 6 and Christ in heaven (Part 4), and the early church (Part 5).

1. Jesus’ words: “This is my body”

“Take, eat; this is my body” Matthew 26:26–28

26Now as they were eating, Jesus took bread, and after blessing it broke it and gave it to the disciples, and said, “Take, eat; this is my body.” 27And he took a cup, and when he had given thanks he gave it to them, saying, “Drink of it, all of you, 28for this is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins.ESV

See it in context

What the LCMS says

“The words of institution, ‘Take, eat; this is My body,’ clearly state: ‘With this bread I give you My body.’ So these words are explained 1 Co 10:16.”

LCMS, Christian Cyclopedia (Concordia Publishing House), “Grace, Means of,” IV, “Lord’s Supper as a means of grace,” section 3 (Real Presence)

“The clear claim of Christ in Holy Scripture is that His true body and blood are truly present and distributed to those who eat. All four Scriptural accounts (Matt. 26:26-29; Mark 14:22-25; Luke 22:15-20; 1 Cor. 11:23-26) assert: ‘This is my body.’ ‘This is my blood of the covenant’ (Matthew and Mark). ‘This cup is the new covenant in my blood’ (Luke and Paul).”

LCMS CTCR, Theology and Practice of the Lord’s Supper (1983), Part I, C.1

“Now here stands the Word of Christ: Take, eat; this is My body; Drink ye all of it; this is the new testament in My blood, etc. Here we abide”

What Lutherans confess: Large Catechism, The Sacrament of the Altar, paragraph 13

Note The “words of institution” are Jesus’ words when He gave the Supper. “1 Co” is 1 Corinthians: the Cyclopedia means that Paul explains these words in 1 Corinthians 10:16 (Part 2).

Added fact All four accounts in the Greek New Testament use the ordinary word for “is” (estin) in “This is my body.” The same word also appears in sentences most readers take as figures, so the word alone does not settle the question.

Objection“Jesus also said ‘I am the door’ and ‘I am the vine.’ Isn’t ‘This is my body’ a figure of speech too?”
The LCMS answers

“Lutheran Christians firmly confess that the ‘true’ body and blood—that is, the real, actual body and blood of our Lord Jesus Christ—are what Christ gives Christians to eat and to drink. Any talk of Jesus’ words as symbolic or mere figures of speech is ruled out.”

LCMS CTCR, Proper Administration of the Lord’s Supper (2025), Theological Considerations

“For since Christ gives this command [concerning eating His body, etc.] at the table and at supper, there is indeed no doubt that He speaks of real, natural bread and of natural wine … Likewise, also Christ Himself takes care that there be no metonymy either, that is, that in the same manner there be no change of meaning in the word body, and that He does not speak concerning a sign of His body, or concerning an emblem [a symbol] or figurative body … but of His true, essential body, which He delivered into death for us”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraphs 48–49

“here, as in the making of His last will and testament and of His everabiding covenant and union … He uses not allegorical, but entirely proper, simple, indubitable, and clear words; and in order that no misunderstanding can occur, He explains them more clearly with the words: Given for you, shed for you.”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 50

“yet they have received the words of Christ properly and as they read, and have understood the proposition, that is, the words of Christ’s testament: Hoc est corpus meum, This is My body, not as a figuratam propositionem, but inusitatam (that is, not as a figurative, allegorical expression or comment, but as an unusual expression).”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 38

Note The Formula’s reason is the setting. Jesus spoke at a meal, handed the disciples bread and wine, told them to eat and drink, and added “given for you” and “shed for you.” So it calls “This is my body” not a figure of speech but “an unusual expression”: a gift with nothing to compare it to. “They” in the last quote are Luther and the Lutheran teachers. “Metonymy” is a figure in which one word stands for something else, as “the crown” can mean the king. “Indubitable” means beyond doubt.

Not addressed None of these documents takes up “I am the door” or “I am the vine” by name.

Objection“In the Bible ‘is’ often means ‘represents’: ‘The seven good cows are seven years’ (Genesis 41:26); ‘the Rock was Christ’ (1 Corinthians 10:4). Even at the Passover, the meal Jesus was keeping, the lamb is called ‘the Lord’s Passover’ (Exodus 12:11).”
The LCMS answers

“We believe, teach, and confess that the words of the testament of Christ are not to be understood otherwise than as they read, according to the letter, so that the bread does not signify the absent body and the wine the absent blood of Christ, but that, on account of the sacramental union, they [the bread and wine] are truly the body and blood of Christ.”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Epitome VII, Affirmative Thesis 2 (paragraph 7)

“Any effort to make the ‘This is’ something less than a clear word, as Reformed theology does by denying the real presence of the body and blood of Christ on earth, is a departure from Christ’s words.”

LCMS CTCR, Theology and Practice of the Lord’s Supper (1983), Part I, C.1

Note The “testament” is Jesus’ last will: words spoken the night before He died. “Reformed” churches are those in the line of Zwingli and Calvin (see “The four main views” near the top).

Not addressed These documents do not discuss Genesis 41:26, 1 Corinthians 10:4, or the Passover wording.

Added fact Each side points to context. In Genesis 41:25–26 Joseph is interpreting Pharaoh’s dream (Genesis 41:15–16), and in 1 Corinthians 10 Paul says the events “took place as examples for us” (1 Corinthians 10:6). Those who hold the memorial view answer that the Passover meal itself was full of explained symbols (Exodus 12:26–27), and that the Lord’s Supper is read the same way.

Objection“Jesus was sitting at the table in His own body. How could the bread in His hands also be His body?”
The LCMS answers

“it is wrong to reject Christ’s clear words simply because our fallen human reason cannot fully understand how it comes to pass.”

LCMS CTCR, Theology and Practice of the Lord’s Supper (1983), Part I, C.1

“On the contrary, in accord with the simple words of Christ’s testament, we hold and believe in a true, though supernatural, eating of Christ’s body and drinking of his blood, which we cannot comprehend with our human sense or reason. Here we take our intellect captive in obedience to Christ, as we do in other articles also, and accept this mystery in no other way than by faith and as it is revealed in the Word (FC Ep VII, 42).”

LCMS CTCR, Theology and Practice of the Lord’s Supper (1983), Part I, C.5, quoting the Formula of Concord

“Secondly, the incomprehensible, spiritual mode, according to which He neither occupies nor vacates space, but penetrates all creatures wherever He pleases [according to His most free will] … This mode He used when He rose from the closed [and sealed] sepulcher, and passed through the closed door [to His disciples], and in the bread and wine in the Holy Supper”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 100, quoting Luther

Note Luther is describing ways Christ’s one body can be present. The first is the ordinary way, as He walked on earth. This is the second. The risen Jesus came to His disciples “although the doors were locked” (John 20:19). Lutherans do not claim to explain how Christ is present, only that He said so.

Objection“After saying ‘This is my blood,’ Jesus still called it ‘this fruit of the vine’ (Matthew 26:29).”
The LCMS answers

Note Lutherans agree that it is still wine. The same Cyclopedia paragraph cites Matthew 26:29 when it says:

“The heavenly elements in the Sacrament are the true body and the true blood of Christ; the earthly elements are true bread and true wine”

LCMS, Christian Cyclopedia (Concordia Publishing House), “Grace, Means of,” IV, “Lord’s Supper as a means of grace,” section 4 (Elements in the Sacrament)

“For it is in perfect agreement with Holy Scriptures that there is, and remains, bread, as Paul himself calls it, 1 Cor. 10:16: The bread which we break. And 1 Cor. 11:28: Let him so eat of that bread.”

What Lutherans confess: Smalcald Articles, Part III, Article VI, paragraph 5

Note So the Lutheran view is not that the bread and wine stop being bread and wine. That is the Roman Catholic teaching of transubstantiation, which Lutherans reject (see “The four main views” near the top).

“This is my body… Do this in remembrance of me” Luke 22:19–20

19And he took bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke it and gave it to them, saying, “This is my body, which is given for you. Do this in remembrance of me.” 20And likewise the cup after they had eaten, saying, “This cup that is poured out for you is the new covenant in my blood.ESV

See it in context

What the LCMS says

Note Here “This is my body, which is given for you” and “Do this in remembrance of me” come one right after the other. Lutherans hold that both are true.

“The words ‘This do in remembrance of Me’ do not mean merely that the communicant is to remember the absent Christ, who atoned for his sins; they invite the communicant to accept the forgiveness offered in the Sacrament (‘Do this in remembrance of Me’ means: remember Christ’s blessings and accept them by faith; cf. Ap XXIV 72).”

LCMS, Christian Cyclopedia (Concordia Publishing House), “Grace, Means of,” IV, “Lord’s Supper as a means of grace,” section 5 (Purpose of the Lord's Supper)

“to remember Christ … is to remember the benefits of Christ and receive them by faith, so as to be quickened by them.”

What Lutherans confess: Apology of the Augsburg Confession, Article XXIV, paragraph 72

Note A “communicant” is a person receiving Communion. “Quickened” means made alive. “Ap XXIV 72” is the second quote. Many who hold the memorial view would agree with it.

Objection“Jesus said, ‘Do this in remembrance of me.’ Doesn’t that make it a memorial?”
The LCMS answers

“this most venerable Sacrament, which was to be used until the end of the world with great reverence and obedience [and humility], and was to be an abiding memorial of His bitter suffering and death and all His benefits, a sealing [and confirmation] of the New Testament, a consolation of all distressed hearts, and a firm bond of union of Christians with Christ, their Head, and with one another”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 44

“Thus the Lord’s Supper was instituted in the church so that as this sign reminds us of the promises of Christ, the remembrance might strengthen our faith and we might publicly confess our faith and announce the blessings of Christ, as Paul says (1 Cor. 11:26), ‘As often as you do this, you proclaim the Lord’s death’ (Ap IV, 210).”

LCMS CTCR, Theology and Practice of the Lord’s Supper (1983), Part I, E.3, quoting the Apology of the Augsburg Confession

Note So the LCMS does not deny that the Supper is a remembrance. It calls it an “abiding memorial of His bitter suffering and death” (paragraph 44), and says it is also what Christ’s words call it: His body and blood, given for the forgiveness of sins.

Objection“We remember someone who is absent. Jesus said, ‘You will not always have me’ (Matthew 26:11), and the Supper is kept ‘until he comes’ (1 Corinthians 11:26).”
The LCMS answers

“St. Paul also links the institution of the Lord’s Supper with the second coming of Christ: ‘For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes’ (1 Cor.11:26). Thus the communicant confesses or ‘proclaims’ confident faith in the Lord’s promised return when he partakes of the Lord’s Supper.”

LCMS CTCR, Theology and Practice of the Lord’s Supper (1983), Part I, C.3

Note Lutherans agree that Christ is no longer with us visibly, as He was before His ascension, and that He will return visibly. They hold that He is still truly present where He promised to be. See the Cyclopedia quote on this card (“not … merely … the absent Christ”) and the objection about Christ in heaven (Part 4).

Not addressed These documents do not discuss Matthew 26:11, or the Greek and Hebrew words for “remembrance.”

Objection“‘This cup is the new covenant in my blood’ can’t be literal: a cup is not a covenant. So ‘This is my body’ is figurative too.”
The LCMS answers

“Therefore there is no doubt that also concerning the other part of the Sacrament these words of Luke and Paul: This cup is the new testament in My blood, can have no other meaning than that which St. Matthew and St. Mark give: This (namely, that which you orally drink out of the cup) is My blood of the new testament, whereby I establish, seal, and confirm with you men this My testament and new covenant, namely, the forgiveness of sins.”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraphs 52–53

Note So the Formula reads Luke’s and Paul’s wording by Matthew’s and Mark’s: what is in the cup is Christ’s blood, and with it He gives the new covenant, the forgiveness of sins. Lutherans agree that “cup” here means what is in the cup. Compare Matthew 26:27–28.

Not addressed These documents do not explain why a figure of speech is accepted in “cup” but not in “is” or “body.”

2. Paul’s explanation

“A participation in the body of Christ” 1 Corinthians 10:16–17

16The cup of blessing that we bless, is it not a participation in the blood of Christ? The bread that we break, is it not a participation in the body of Christ? 17Because there is one bread, we who are many are one body, for we all partake of the one bread.ESV

See it in context

What the LCMS says

“For since Paul says, 1 Cor. 10:16, that the bread is the communion of the Lord’s body, etc., it would follow, if the Lord’s body were not truly present, that the bread is not a communion of the body, but only of the spirit of Christ.”

What Lutherans confess: Apology of the Augsburg Confession, Article X, paragraph 54

“From this we clearly learn that not only the cup which Christ blessed at the first Supper, and not only the bread which Christ broke and distributed, but also that which we break and bless, is the communion of the body and blood of Christ, so that all who eat this bread and drink of this cup truly receive, and are partakers of, the true body and blood of Christ.”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 54

Added fact The Greek word here, koinōnia, means sharing, participation, or fellowship. Translations give “participation” (ESV, NIV), “sharing” (NASB), or “communion” (KJV, NKJV). Change the translation at the top of the page to compare.

Objection“‘Participation’ means fellowship with Christ by faith, or with the church, which verse 17 calls one body.”
The LCMS answers

“the two passages in which Paul teaches about (or relies on teaching about) the Lord’s Supper (1 Cor. 10:16–18; 1 Cor. 11:17–34) primarily emphasize the corporate character and implications of the Eucharist. … Paul explains why the Corinthian Christians cannot partake of the sacrifices at idol shrines while also eating the Lord’s Supper. After establishing that their eating and drinking is, in fact, participation in the body and the blood of the Lord (10:16), Paul continues: ‘Because there is one loaf, we many are one body, for we all partake of the one loaf’ (10:17).”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part I, A

“And if Paul were speaking only of the spiritual communion of the body of Christ through faith … he would not say that the bread, but that the spirit or faith, was the communion of the body of Christ.”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 56

“For since all those who become partakers of the consecrated and broken bread in the Supper have communion also with the body of Christ, St. Paul indeed cannot be speaking of spiritual communion with Christ, which no man can abuse, and against which also no one is to be warned.”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 57

Note So the LCMS agrees that the church is in view here: “corporate” means having to do with the whole body of believers. It reads verse 16 as Christ’s body given with the bread, and verse 17 as the result: those who share it are one body. The Formula’s point is that Paul warns against eating wrongly (at idol feasts as well, 1 Corinthians 10:20–21), and faith in Christ, it says, cannot be misused that way.

“Guilty concerning the body and blood of the Lord” 1 Corinthians 11:27–29

27Whoever, therefore, eats the bread or drinks the cup of the Lord in an unworthy manner will be guilty concerning the body and blood of the Lord. 28Let a person examine himself, then, and so eat of the bread and drink of the cup. 29For anyone who eats and drinks without discerning the body eats and drinks judgment on himself.ESV

See it in context

What the LCMS says

“In, with, and under the bread and wine a communicant, also an unbelieving communicant (1 Co 11:27–29), receives Christ’s true body, given into death, and His true blood, shed for sins.”

LCMS, Christian Cyclopedia (Concordia Publishing House), “Grace, Means of,” IV, “Lord’s Supper as a means of grace,” section 3 (Real Presence)

“‘discerning the body’ requires faith that Christ’s true body and blood are received in, with, and under the eucharistic bread and wine. This is a sine qua non for all discussion of Paul’s theology and understanding of the Lord’s Supper. We do not actually know if some of the Corinthians were denying this teaching outright. But if they did not deny it de jure, they were denying it de facto by their schisms and factions (11:18–19), for their behavior contradicted the promised benefit of the Lord’s Supper and brought divine judgment upon some of their number.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part I, A

Note “Sine qua non” is Latin for something essential, “without which, nothing.” “De jure” means formally, as a stated belief, and “de facto” in practice. So the 1999 report says that the sin Paul names at Corinth was division in the congregation (1 Corinthians 11:18–22), which it treats as a denial of the real presence in practice. It says it is not known whether anyone there denied it outright. “Eucharistic” means of the Lord’s Supper.

Objection“Dishonoring a symbol can still dishonor what it stands for, like burning a flag. Paul’s warning doesn’t prove Christ’s body is present.”
Related LCMS passages

Not addressed These documents do not discuss this comparison. The related passage is the Formula’s reading of 1 Corinthians 11:27:

“For he says, 1 Cor. 11:27: Whosoever shall eat this bread, and drink this cup of the Lord, unworthily, sins not merely against the bread and wine, not merely against the signs or symbols and emblems of the body and blood, but shall be guilty of the body and blood of the Lord Jesus Christ, which, as there [in the Holy Supper] present, he dishonors, abuses, and disgraces”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 60

Note “He” is Paul. So the Formula reads “guilty of the body and blood” as a sin against what is present, not against signs. The flag comparison is a fair question to talk through.

↑ Back to the list

Objection“‘Discerning the body’ in verse 29 means recognizing the church as Christ’s body.”
The LCMS answers

“The most natural direction to look for help in determining the referent of ‘the body’ in verse 29 is back to verses 27 and 28.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part I, A

“in the places in 1 Corinthians where the noun ‘body’ does refer directly and unambiguously to the church as the body of Christ, there are always explicit textual markers that indicate that reference.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part I, A

“The strongest case, then, can be made for a eucharistic reference of ‘the body’ at 11:29. The Corinthian Christians have not been ‘discerning the eucharistic presence of Christ’s body and blood.’ Having argued this, however, is that all that we might say? Is it possible that ‘discerning the body’ also and at the same time involves a ‘subtle allusion’ to the church which gathers around the Sacrament?”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Part I, A

Note The report answers its own question yes: the church is also in view, but not instead of Christ’s body in the Supper. Verses 27 and 28 speak of “the bread” and “the body and blood of the Lord.” The “referent” is what a word refers to. An “allusion” is an indirect reference.

↑ Back to the list

3. What the Supper gives

Note In the memorial view, the Supper strengthens faith as believers remember and proclaim Christ’s death. The LCMS agrees, and says Christ also gives something in it. These are its words for what that is.

“That is shown us in these words: Given, and shed for you, for the remission of sins; namely, that in the Sacrament forgiveness of sins, life, and salvation are given us through these words. For where there is forgiveness of sins, there is also life and salvation.”

What Lutherans confess: Small Catechism, The Sacrament of the Altar, “What is the benefit of such eating and drinking?”

“The Lord’s Supper is essentially an application of the Gospel, with all its spiritual blessings, in a sacred act. It offers, conveys, and seals to the communicant forgiveness of sins, life, and salvation; strengthens faith”

LCMS, Christian Cyclopedia (Concordia Publishing House), “Grace, Means of,” IV, “Lord’s Supper as a means of grace,” section 5 (Purpose of the Lord's Supper)

Note “Remission” means forgiveness.

“A single offering for sins” Hebrews 10:10–14

10And by that will we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all. 11And every priest stands daily at his service, offering repeatedly the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins. 12But when Christ had offered for all time a single sacrifice for sins, he sat down at the right hand of God, 13waiting from that time until his enemies should be made a footstool for his feet. 14For by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified.ESV

See it in context

What the LCMS says

“By His work of atonement the Lord sacrificed Himself for the sins of all people. This action was completed in its entirety when our Lord uttered the words ‘It is finished!’”

LCMS CTCR, Theology and Practice of the Lord’s Supper (1983), Part I, C.2

“In the words of institution Jesus openly asserts that His blood is being ‘poured out’ (Matt. 26:28; Mark 14:24; Luke 22:20) and that His body is being ‘given’ (Luke 22:19). Both terms underscore the sacrificial nature of His death. … Jesus now presents His body and blood in bread and wine as the means of divine grace ‘for the forgiveness of sins’ (Matt. 26:28). With His body and blood in the sacrament, He thereby bestows all the blessings and benefits of the atonement (Heb. 9:14-16).”

LCMS CTCR, Theology and Practice of the Lord’s Supper (1983), Part I, C.2

Note So in the Lutheran view the cross is where forgiveness was won, once for all, and the Supper is one of the ways it is given out. The “atonement” is Christ’s death for our sins.

Objection“If Christ’s body is offered on the altar, isn’t that sacrificing Him again? Hebrews says once for all.”
The LCMS answers

“Therefore let this remain established in the case, namely, that the death of Christ alone is truly a propitiatory sacrifice.”

What Lutherans confess: Apology of the Augsburg Confession, Article XXIV, paragraph 24

Note A “propitiatory sacrifice” is one that atones for sin. So Lutherans agree: Christ is not sacrificed again. In the Lutheran view He gives in the Supper the body once given on the cross.

Added fact Roman Catholics teach that the Mass makes present the one sacrifice of the cross rather than repeating it. Lutherans still reject the Mass as a sacrifice for sins, as the Apology does at length.

↑ Back to the list

Objection“‘For the forgiveness of sins’ (Matthew 26:28) describes what Christ’s blood did on the cross. It does not mean the Supper itself gives forgiveness.”
The LCMS answers

“Jesus now presents His body and blood in bread and wine as the means of divine grace ‘for the forgiveness of sins’ (Matt. 26:28).”

LCMS CTCR, Theology and Practice of the Lord’s Supper (1983), Part I, C.2

“Now this is plain and clear from the words just mentioned: This is My body and blood, given and shed for you, for the remission of sins. Briefly that is as much as to say: For this reason we go to the Sacrament because there we receive such a treasure by and in which we obtain forgiveness of sins.”

What Lutherans confess: Large Catechism, The Sacrament of the Altar, paragraphs 21–22

Note Lutherans agree that forgiveness was won on the cross, once for all. Their claim is that Christ gives it out in the Supper, to each one, “for you.” In the words of the Small Catechism at the top of this part, forgiveness, life, and salvation “are given us through these words” in the Sacrament.

Not addressed These documents do not discuss the reading that the phrase describes only the cross.

↑ Back to the list

Objection“The Small Catechism itself says that whoever believes the words has forgiveness. Then what does Christ’s body in the bread add?”
The LCMS answers

“It is not the eating and drinking, indeed, that does them, but the words which stand here, namely: Given, and shed for you, for the remission of sins. Which words are, beside the bodily eating and drinking, as the chief thing in the Sacrament; and he that believes these words has what they say and express, namely, the forgiveness of sins.”

What Lutherans confess: Small Catechism, The Sacrament of the Altar, “How can bodily eating and drinking do such great things?”

“for on this account He bids me eat and drink, that it may be my own and may benefit me, as a sure pledge and token, yea, the very same treasure that is appointed for me against my sins, death, and every calamity.”

What Lutherans confess: Large Catechism, The Sacrament of the Altar, paragraph 22

Note “Them” means forgiveness, life, and salvation. So Lutherans agree that faith receives the gift and that the eating alone does nothing. They hold that Christ gives His body and blood as a personal pledge, “for you,” of the forgiveness He also promises in His Word.

↑ Back to the list

4. John 6 and Christ in heaven

“Whoever feeds on my flesh and drinks my blood” John 6:53–56

53So Jesus said to them, “Truly, truly, I say to you, unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you. 54Whoever feeds on my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life, and I will raise him up on the last day. 55For my flesh is true food, and my blood is true drink. 56Whoever feeds on my flesh and drinks my blood abides in me, and I in him.ESV

See it in context

What the LCMS says

Note This may surprise some readers: the Lutheran Confessions read John 6 as chiefly about faith, as many who hold the memorial view do. Lutherans rest their teaching on the Supper on Jesus’ words at the Supper and on Paul.

“There is, therefore, a two-fold eating of the flesh of Christ, one spiritual, of which Christ treats especially John 6:54, which occurs in no other way than with the Spirit and faith, in the preaching and meditation of the Gospel, as well as in the Lord’s Supper, and by itself is useful and salutary, and necessary at all times for salvation to all Christians”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 61

“But this spiritual eating is nothing else than faith, namely, to hear God’s Word … to receive it with faith and appropriate it to ourselves”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 62

Note “Salutary” means bringing salvation. The “other” eating, in the Supper, is in the last objection of this part.

Objection“Jesus said, ‘The flesh is no help at all’ (John 6:63). Even if His flesh were eaten with the mouth, it would do nothing; only the Spirit and faith give life. So a bodily presence would serve no purpose.”
Related LCMS passages

Note Lutherans agree that John 6 speaks of eating Christ by faith (see the quotes above).

“Now the body of Christ can never be an unfruitful, vain thing, that effects or profits nothing.”

What Lutherans confess: Large Catechism, The Sacrament of the Altar, paragraph 30

Not addressed These documents do not discuss John 6:63 itself.

Added fact The Greek says “the flesh,” not “my flesh”: hē sarx ouk ōphelei ouden. Many Lutheran teachers read it as human nature without the Spirit, not as a statement that Christ’s own flesh is useless. That reading is not from these documents.

↑ Back to the list

Objection“Eating Christ’s flesh and drinking His blood sounds like cannibalism. The disciples were Jews, forbidden to drink blood (Leviticus 17:10–12), yet none of them objected.”
The LCMS answers

“when at the table and during the Supper He offers His disciples natural bread and natural wine, which He calls His true body and true blood, at the same time saying: Eat and drink. For in view of the circumstances this command evidently cannot be understood otherwise than of oral eating and drinking, however, not in a gross, carnal, Capernaitic, but in a supernatural, incomprehensible way”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 64

“Bread and wine are received in a natural manner; the body and blood of Christ, though received orally, are received in an incomprehensible, supernatural manner (no Capernaitic eating; FC SD VII 64).”

LCMS, Christian Cyclopedia (Concordia Publishing House), “Grace, Means of,” IV, “Lord’s Supper as a means of grace,” section 4 (Elements in the Sacrament)

Note “Oral” means with the mouth. “Capernaitic” eating means chewing Christ’s flesh like ordinary meat, as some of Jesus’ hearers at Capernaum understood Him (John 6:52). Lutherans reject that too.

Not addressed These documents do not discuss the law against drinking blood, or why the disciples did not object.

↑ Back to the list

“Seated him at his right hand… far above all rule” Ephesians 1:20–23

20that he worked in Christ when he raised him from the dead and seated him at his right hand in the heavenly places, 21far above all rule and authority and power and dominion, and above every name that is named, not only in this age but also in the one to come. 22And he put all things under his feet and gave him as head over all things to the church, 23which is his body, the fullness of him who fills all in all.ESV

See it in context

What the LCMS says

“That God’s right hand is everywhere; at which Christ is placed in deed and in truth according to His human nature, [and therefore] being present, rules, and has in His hands and beneath His feet everything that is in heaven and on earth”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Epitome VII, Affirmative Thesis 5 (paragraph 12), the second of four reasons from Luther, citing Ephesians 1:22

“Thus our faith in this article concerning the true presence of the body and blood of Christ in the Holy Supper is based upon the truth and omnipotence of the true, almighty God, our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ.”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 106

Note So Lutherans read “God’s right hand” as God’s rule over all things, as in this passage, not as one place in heaven. Luther’s four reasons are that Christ is God and man in one person, that God’s right hand is everywhere, that God’s Word does not lie, and that God has more than one way of being present. “Omnipotence” means being all-powerful.

Objection“Christ’s body is a true human body like ours, and a human body is in one place. He ascended, and ‘heaven must receive him until the time for restoring all things’ (Acts 3:21). So His body cannot be in the bread.”
The LCMS answers

“First, the comprehensible, bodily mode, as He went about bodily upon earth, when, according to His size, He vacated and occupied space [was circumscribed by a fixed place]. This mode He can still use whenever He will, as He did after the resurrection, and will use at the last day … In this manner He is not in God or with the Father, neither in heaven … for God is not a bodily space or place. And this is what the passages how Christ leaves the world and goes to the Father refer to”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 99, quoting Luther

“That the body of Christ is so enclosed in heaven that it can in no way be at once and at one time in many or all places upon earth where His Holy Supper is celebrated.”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Epitome VII, Negative Thesis 11 (paragraph 32), a teaching it rejects

Note So Lutherans agree that Christ has a true human body, ascended, and will return visibly. Luther says the Bible passages about Christ leaving the world and going to the Father speak of this first, bodily way of being present. He holds that Christ’s body can also be present in other ways (see the objection “Jesus was right there at the table”). The Formula of Concord takes up Acts 3:21 itself (Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 119) and argues over how to translate it.

Added fact The Greek of Acts 3:21 can be read “whom heaven must receive” or “who must take possession of heaven.” Most English translations give the first.

↑ Back to the list

Objection“By the Holy Spirit, believers truly feed on Christ’s body and blood in the Supper, but an unbeliever receives only bread.”
The LCMS answers

“The other eating of the body of Christ is oral or sacramental, when the true, essential body and blood of Christ are also orally received and partaken of in the Holy Supper, by all who eat and drink the consecrated bread and wine in the Supper—by the believing as a certain pledge and assurance that their sins are surely forgiven them, and Christ dwells and is efficacious in them, but by the unbelieving for their judgment and condemnation”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 63

“In this sense we also say [wish the word spiritually to be understood when we say] that in the Holy Supper the body and blood of Christ are spiritually received, eaten, and drunk, although this participation occurs with the mouth, while the mode is spiritual.”

What Lutherans confess: Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII, paragraph 105

Note This is the view of Calvin and the Reformed churches. Lutherans agree with much of it (see “Where we agree” at the top). They too call the presence “spiritual,” meaning heavenly and beyond our senses. The difference is that they say Christ’s body is received with the bread, by the mouth, by everyone who receives the Supper, as Paul’s warning in 1 Corinthians 11:27 implies (Part 2). “Efficacious” means at work.

5. What the early Christians said

Note These writers lived after the apostles. Their words are evidence of what early Christians believed, not proof from Scripture. They are not LCMS documents. The dates and remarks in the lines marked “Added fact” are the page’s own. Both sides cite early writers.

“They abstain from the Eucharist and from prayer, because they confess not the Eucharist to be the flesh of our Saviour Jesus Christ, which suffered for our sins, and which the Father, of His goodness, raised up again.”

Ignatius of Antioch, Letter to the Smyrnaeans, chapter 7, about AD 110. Not an LCMS document

Added fact Ignatius was bishop of Antioch and died a martyr in Rome. By early tradition he knew the apostle John. He was writing about people who denied that Jesus had real flesh at all, which those who hold the memorial view also reject.

“For not as common bread and common drink do we receive these; but in like manner as Jesus Christ our Saviour, having been made flesh by the Word of God, had both flesh and blood for our salvation, so likewise have we been taught that the food which is blessed by the prayer of His word … is the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh.”

Justin Martyr, First Apology, chapter 66, about AD 155. Not an LCMS document

Added fact Justin wrote this to explain Christian worship to the Roman emperor.

“When, therefore, the mingled cup and the manufactured bread receives the Word of God, and the Eucharist of the blood and the body of Christ is made, from which things the substance of our flesh is increased and supported, how can they affirm that the flesh is incapable of receiving the gift of God, which is life eternal, which [flesh] is nourished from the body and blood of the Lord, and is a member of Him?”

Irenaeus, Against Heresies, book 5, chapter 2, about AD 180. Not an LCMS document

Added fact Irenaeus, bishop of Lyons, heard Polycarp preach when he was young. He was arguing against teachers who said the body cannot be saved.

“Consider therefore the Bread and the Wine not as bare elements, for they are, according to the Lord’s declaration, the Body and Blood of Christ; for even though sense suggests this to you, yet let faith establish you.”

Catechetical Lecture 22, traditionally by Cyril of Jerusalem, AD 350–390. Not an LCMS document

Added fact These lectures, for people who had just been baptized, are traditionally credited to Cyril, bishop of Jerusalem. Some scholars assign them to his successor, John.

Writers often cited on the other side

“Then, having taken the bread and given it to His disciples, He made it His own body, by saying, This is my body, that is, the figure of my body. A figure, however, there could not have been, unless there were first a veritable body.”

Tertullian, Against Marcion, book 4, chapter 40, about AD 208. Not an LCMS document

Added fact Tertullian calls the bread “the figure” of Christ’s body. He is arguing against Marcion, who taught that Jesus’ body was not real, and his point is that a figure requires a real body. Scholars disagree about how much “figure” meant to him.

“For if sacraments had not some points of real resemblance to the things of which they are the sacraments, they would not be sacraments at all. In most cases, moreover, they do in virtue of this likeness bear the names of the realities which they resemble. As, therefore, in a certain manner the sacrament of Christ’s body is Christ’s body, and the sacrament of Christ’s blood is Christ’s blood, in the same manner the sacrament of faith is faith.”

Augustine, Letter 98, to Boniface, about AD 408. Not an LCMS document

Added fact Augustine was bishop of Hippo in North Africa. Here he says a sacrament bears the name of what it resembles, yet also that “in a certain manner” the sacrament of Christ’s body is Christ’s body. Both sides cite him. (The “sacrament of faith” is Baptism: he is explaining why an infant is said to believe.)

What the Lutheran Confessions say about the early church

“And we have ascertained that not only the Roman Church affirms the bodily presence of Christ, but the Greek Church also both now believes, and formerly believed, the same.”

What Lutherans confess: Apology of the Augsburg Confession, Article X, paragraph 55

Note The “Greek Church” is the Eastern Orthodox Church. Roman Catholics and the Orthodox explain the presence differently from Lutherans, but they too hold that Christ’s body and blood are truly present.

Figurative views before and during the Reformation

“Ratramnus, asked for his opinion by Charles II of Fr., condemned the treatise of Radbertus and stressed figurative and mysterious aspects of the Sacrament.”

LCMS, Christian Cyclopedia (Concordia Publishing House), “Eucharistic Controversies”

Note This was in the 800s, in France. Ratramnus was a monk who answered another monk, Paschasius Radbertus, who taught that the bread and wine are changed into Christ’s body and blood.

“Ca. 1523 Zwingli adopted C. H. Hoen’s doctrine of the Eucharist.”

LCMS, Christian Cyclopedia (Concordia Publishing House), “Zwingli, Huldreich”

“In 1529, in his only meeting with Luther, he attended the Marburg Colloquy”

LCMS, Christian Cyclopedia (Concordia Publishing House), “Zwingli, Huldreich”

Note “Ca.” means about. Ulrich Zwingli led the Reformation in Zurich, Switzerland. Cornelius Hoen was a Dutch lawyer who argued that “is” in “This is my body” means “signifies.”

Added fact At Marburg, Germany, in 1529, Luther and Zwingli agreed on fourteen of fifteen articles of faith, and on most of the fifteenth. They could not agree on whether Christ’s body and blood are truly present in the bread and wine. Luther is said to have written “This is my body” in chalk on the table.

Added fact Most Baptist and many non-denominational churches today hold the memorial view, which is often traced to Zwingli. Many Presbyterian and other Reformed churches, and some Baptists, hold Calvin’s view of a spiritual presence.

6. Why this shapes where Lutherans commune

Note Because Lutherans believe Christ gives His body and blood in the Supper, they receive it where it is taught that way, and they ask those who commune at their altars to share that confession.

“By rejecting the practice of open Communion, pastors and communing laity alike preserve a unity of confession regarding the Sacrament, namely, that in, with and under the elements Christ is present as He promised for the forgiveness of sins. Pastors may, nevertheless, exercise discretion in special cases or emergencies.”

lcms.org, Beliefs, “Who can commune at a Lutheran Church—Missouri Synod congregation?”

“We do this not with any thought of superiority or with any gladness, but in deep sorrow and prayer to the Lord of the Church that he would remove the divisions that separate His children from one another in their visible expressions of fellowship.”

LCMS CTCR, Admission to the Lord’s Supper (1999), Concluding Summary

Note “Laity” are church members who are not pastors. The second quote is from the 1999 report, on asking Christians of other churches not to commune at LCMS altars. More on this is in the companion page Close Communion: The Verses.

Not answered in these documents

None of the documents quoted on this page takes up these points:

  • Jesus probably spoke Aramaic at the Supper, a language that can leave out “is” (“this my body”), so the Greek “is” may not settle the question.
  • In Genesis 41:26 and 1 Corinthians 10:4 “is” means “represents,” and at the Passover the lamb is called “the Lord’s Passover” (Exodus 12:11), so “is” may mean “represents” in “This is my body” too.
  • Dishonoring a symbol can dishonor what it stands for, so 1 Corinthians 11:27 does not require a real presence.
  • John 6:63, “the flesh is no help at all,” rules out any eating of Christ’s flesh.
  • The disciples, as Jews, were forbidden to drink blood (Leviticus 17:10–12), yet they did not object.
  • A figure of speech is accepted in “cup” (Luke 22:20); why not in “is” or “body”?
  • The Hebrew idea of “remembrance” in a memorial meal like the Passover (Exodus 12:14).

They are fair questions, and worth talking through together.
